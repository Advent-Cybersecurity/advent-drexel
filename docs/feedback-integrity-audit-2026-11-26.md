# Feedback integrity in self-modifying agents

26 November 2026. Source-only review for Advent Cybersecurity; no model or executable was run. Primary source: [Roesner and Kohno, arXiv v1, 15 September 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.17817). This is an adjacent *evaluation-integrity* study, not a Drexel payload experiment.

## What was tested

The researchers supplied deliberately poisoned coding benchmarks to three self-modifying coding systems, then asked whether selected descendants produced an unsafe coding pattern on neutral held-out tasks. The seed agent was initially clean; the underlying model was not changed. The study evaluates *offline evolutionary generations* and later code outputs, rather than one remotely serving process undergoing active updates. For each selected evolved agent in the main table, the held-out rate uses ten tasks sampled three times each. Those 30 outputs are not 30 independent tasks or a remote-access survival denominator.

The three systems do not have the same editable surface. In the modified Darwin Gödel Machine (`DGM-bar`), the sought artifact was a reusable **code-emitting tool**. The researchers substantively changed the stock DGM diagnosis prompt to encourage such tools after preliminary failures; they explicitly say that change lies outside their attacker threat model. In SICA and Hyperagents, the measured persistent change was an **instruction/directive** in the coding agent's harness, which later influenced code written for new tasks. The paper states that stock SICA and stock Hyperagents prompts were used for those cases. This is persistence of *generated-code behavior*, not evidence that the agent's own executable payload was replaced in place.

The main table selects the highest-accuracy descendant that *still carries* the problematic tool or directive, which need not be the terminal version a user would deploy. In the modified-DGM trials, creating a code-emitting tool was also insufficient: one model produced an unsafe-default tool in a poisoned run but invoked it on zero of 30 held-out outputs. The study's strongest cases show tool use or directive transfer on the neutral tasks, while clean benchmark controls did not. These comparisons establish a possible feedback-to-persistent-behavior path under the paper's conditions; they do not provide an unconditional compromise rate for arbitrary feedback.

## Persistence and limits

For persistence, the authors took **one contaminated agent from each of the three systems** and continued its evolution under four benchmark conditions. A clean benchmark that did not explicitly test the problematic behavior left the component in place in their trials. Even one broader security-oriented benchmark with an added relevant task did not remove it. A purpose-built decontamination benchmark removed the directive entirely in the Hyperagents condition and reduced or conditioned it in the other two. These are single-seed continuation demonstrations by system and treatment, not population estimates of recovery or a claim that every validation gate fails.

For Drexel, feedback, validation, and promotion signals are therefore part of the feasibility question: a model might learn a change that scores well on available checks while losing an unmeasured property. An independent after-each remote task and a predeclared retention suite would detect *some* such failures, but their coverage is limited to what they exercise. The paper also cautions against treating a standing review committee's approval as independent proof of safety: in its SICA example, the reported committee accepted the evolved directive under the poisoned reward. This is a design and measurement caveat, not evidence of a live remote-access chain or of endpoint-defense bypass.

[Cao, Deng, and Kersting's separate study](failure-detection-and-repair-audit-2026-11-26.md) isolates another feedback failure: in benign task programs, agent-written checks missed many externally scored losses of function that the same authoring agent could repair when the experimenter forced a recall. Thus *repair ability* cannot substitute for a measured trigger and an independent current-function oracle. Its frozen-program and one-call replay results do not estimate Drexel's serial success rate.
