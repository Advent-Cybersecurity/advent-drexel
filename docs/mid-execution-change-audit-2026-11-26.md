# Mid-execution change: a matched-continuation measurement precedent

26 November 2026. Source-only review of [Huang et al., SWE-Shift, arXiv v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.32196). No models or code were run for Advent Cybersecurity. The study tests coding agents faced with *externally imposed* changes to the workspace or task. It does not test agents changing their own executable harness or a remote-access payload.

## Design and measures

The authors construct 120 task-relevant, recoverable scenarios from 23 task-root snapshots in 17 repositories, grouped into 12 change families. They schedule each scenario at nine intervention positions. For each system/scenario/position, a single execution prefix is saved and forked into two continuations: **Clean** proceeds without the intervention; **Shifted** receives one change. The saved prefix includes visible history, workspace, controller state, tools, settings, and counters. This pairing helps distinguish an agent's baseline inability to solve a task from failure specifically associated with mid-execution change. Model randomness after the fork is not controlled, and some scenarios share roots.

The paper reports Clean and Shifted success, paired success rate (PSR), and conditional retention rate (CRR). PSR requires both continuations to succeed from the matched prefix; CRR is the fraction of Clean successes also successful after the change under the paper's weighting. These definitions are valuable for a future **benign** Drexel analogue: report both the all-starting-instances joint outcome and function after an accepted change conditional on prior function. The study itself has **one external update per run**, so it does not estimate a two-revision sequence or the second step conditional on the first.

Across 17 model–agent systems, each system's Shifted score is lower than its Clean score under these scenarios. For the top Clean performer, the authors report 67.35% Clean, 24.79% Shifted, 20.34% PSR, and 30.20% CRR. These are weighted coding-benchmark metrics with a very large one-million-token post-fork budget, not endpoint or payload reliability numbers. Open-weight systems in the experiment ran on 32 NVIDIA B300 GPUs across four nodes, so their results also cannot describe a small local CPU deployment. In the two change families involving tool/session interfaces and stale evidence, the authors report zero PSR for all 17 systems; this is a property of their designed scenarios and scoring, not a universal impossibility.

## Why the success oracle matters

The qualitative cases show that original task tests can pass while an agent removes valid collaborator work, fails to preserve other workspace state, or relies on an observation from before the final edit. The study's additional preservation and freshness checks catch those conditions. For Drexel, a baseline remote-control task and an after-change task must be bound to the exact accepted version and current process state. A stale response, open socket, or original suite pass would not establish that the revised code handled the new task.

The authors test state-refresh and history controls in five systems. Their full intervention improves PSR by 5.98–8.71 points but leaves final PSR at 10.89–17.54% in that controlled subset. Raising post-fork token budgets eventually shows diminishing gain. These are resource-intensive coding tasks, not evidence that more inference is sufficient or insufficient for a resident implant. The paper explicitly lists repeated changes in natural workflows as future work. Its strongest contribution to Drexel is the **matched-prefix design and current-state oracle**, not a transferable success rate.
