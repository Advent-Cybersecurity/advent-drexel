# Failure detection is a separate part of a revision loop

26 November 2026. Source-only audit of [Cao, Deng, and Kersting, *Blind to What They Can Fix*, arXiv v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.29807), posted 22 November 2026. No model, program, endpoint, or payload was run for Advent Cybersecurity. This study uses benign games and service/application simulations; its outcomes are not remote-access or malware rates.

## Study design and denominator

In five domains, agents first write task programs and, in some formats, their own runtime checks. An improvement loop retains versions for task score, while the authoring agent configuration stays fixed. The investigators then freeze each program and apply designed environmental changes that the agent did not see during development. A program–change pair is a failure when its mean performance across ten deployment episodes falls below half its unmodified-environment score. Detection and repair are measured on the *same* failure pairs. The agent is deliberately recalled on missed failures for the repair test, even where its own check would never have called it back in deployment.

The standard agent-written checks miss at least half of failures in four of five domains in the paper's point estimates: 52% of 168 Seaquest failures for 20 Codex-authored programs, 55% of 22 Kangaroo failures for six, 64% of 25 service-autoscaling failures for four, and 89% of 28 AppWorld failures for four. Craftax is the small exception: no misses among eight failures of three programs. These are **failure-pair** denominators, not independent deployments or two-revision outcomes. In a new-program replication, standard checks missed 69% of 90 Seaquest failures and 95% of 112 AppWorld failures. The authors revised the initial AppWorld failure criterion after seeing too few failures, then registered the new rule for the replication.

When separately invoked on silent failures, the same Codex author repaired 69/77 Seaquest, 37/37 autoscaling, and 55/55 AppWorld cases under the study's recovery threshold. This establishes that a missed trigger can be a distinct bottleneck from repair ability in these tasks; it does **not** show autonomous recovery, because the experimenter forced those recalls. The general monitor, built from normal-run records, reduced misses in several domains, but it also raised many unnecessary alarms and performed poorly in AppWorld. Explicitly telling the author that a check is what will call it back reduced misses on newly authored programs to 6% of 84 Seaquest failures and 0% of 122 AppWorld failures; the authors' uncertainty intervals and small independent-program counts limit generalization.

## Drexel relevance and boundary

This is a useful distinction for any prospective benign two-revision study: record (1) whether the system detects a genuine loss of task function, (2) whether it proposes and accepts a repair when called, (3) whether the repaired code becomes active, and (4) whether an independent task succeeds afterward. A successful repair under forced notification cannot be counted as autonomous maintenance. A monitor must have a measurable current outcome or trustworthy proxy; model-authored checks may not observe the relations needed to identify loss of function. A candidate's own editable success signal is insufficient as the sole oracle.

The reported programs are frozen for the changed-environment failure evaluation and are not a continuing remote-access process. The deployment-value analysis replays recorded sessions with at most one call. Outside Seaquest, few independently authored programs have checks and the change catalogues are small. The study says the general monitor has normal-run reference data unavailable to the program's own checks and may react to harmless changes. No published result here joins two model-caused active payload-code revisions and access tests in one process.
