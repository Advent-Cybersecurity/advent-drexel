# Drexel feasibility: additional public evidence through 25 November 2026

This is a source review for Advent Cybersecurity. No model was run, no malware was obtained or executed, and no new performance measurement was made. It supplements the [master evidence assessment](master-feasibility-evidence.md). The narrow test remains whether **one continuing remote-access program applies at least two model-caused payload-code revisions and retains independently checked access after each revision**. Autonomous commands, new scripts, separate builds, model-weight changes, and operator-side repairs address related but different questions.

## Newly reviewed operational evidence

| Primary source | Published observation | Relevance and limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Unit 42, *Recent Trends and Tactics of Agentic AI Attacks*, 24 November 2026](https://unit42.paloaltonetworks.com/autonomous-ai-agent-threat-campaigns/) | Investigators report more than ten campaigns observed between July and September 2026. Initial footholds in the analyzed set were web shells. One case involved about 800 unique commands over 140 active minutes across two days; another had over 8,000 across twelve days. The report describes 40–100 unique encoded script payloads in a single attack session and iterative reaction to failures. | Strong field evidence for fast, environment-specific script creation and adaptive external operation. The authors state that they recovered **no compiled backdoor, custom C2 framework, DLL implant, or shellcode loader** in this campaign set. The web shells and single-use scripts do not establish two revisions inside one continuing resident program, nor access checks after each. The command and payload counts are from different examples and cannot be combined into a single sequence. |
| [Unit 42, *Attackers Use Autonomous AI to Target Semiconductor, Defense and Civil Society Sectors*, 12 November 2026](https://unit42.paloaltonetworks.com/cairn-framework-ai-autonomous-attacks/) | The investigators attribute 25 Cairn framework operations against at least 19 organizations in August–September 2026 to one campaign and report unauthorized access or data retrieval at ten organizations. The framework used model-directed planning and action from an external controller, with human guidance possible. | Strong evidence for model-mediated multi-stage intrusion in a specified field campaign; the ten-organization figure is an outcome count, not a rate of self-revision or retained access after payload changes. The report does not describe a model modifying the code of a continuing implant. Its relationship to Unit 42's broader campaign review is not quantified, so the totals must not be added. |
| [GTIG, *Agentic Threat Activity Crosses a Significant Threshold*, 16 November 2026](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/agentic-threat-activity-crosses-a-significant-threshold) | GTIG and Mandiant report offensive frameworks used in intrusions and describe an exposed staging server containing `exploit_agent`, which integrates frontier and local models for automated vulnerability research and execution. The server also contained victim data and post-compromise scripts. They observed CAIRN in intrusion activity and a customized PentAGI-based platform used for reconnaissance and scanning. | Advances the evidence for automated, model-mediated external orchestration and for both hosted and local model options. The article describes the framework's setup and artifacts, not a time-ordered record of two successful self-revisions by one resident implant. Its examples are not a denominator for the success rate of such revisions. |
| [GTIG and Mandiant, *ARTEX and Friends*, 30 October 2026](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/artex-and-friends-enter-agentic-offensive-frameworks) | Already in the repository. The report documents rapid agent-driven candidate repair and separate observations of command and control during intrusions. | The earlier [claim audit](master-feasibility-evidence.md#source-quality-cautions) correctly keeps external web-shell replacements, script repairs, and C2 observations in their respective incidents. The two newer reports broaden the field evidence, but do not supply the missing same-instance chain. |

Unit 42's title and tags include remote-access terminology, but its explicit negative finding about recovered compiled implants matters more for this particular hypothesis. Its observed web shells offered continuing access, while the newly generated scripts were distinct short-lived artifacts. In one example it observed two versions of a WMI backdoor with comments in different languages; the article does not link those versions to a model output, a single running process, or after-each access tests. This is evidence of **artifact variation**, not a measured self-revision chain. Unit 42 also reports no two identical payloads across victims and no matches to known tool signatures in that collection. This is a statement about the recovered artifacts, not proof that behavioral controls failed or that generated malware is generally undetectable.

GTIG explicitly says the currently observed autonomous frameworks trade stealth for speed: high-volume activity and code-level traces leave forensic evidence. That aligns with Unit 42's behavioral fingerprints. Neither source publishes a controlled detection rate against a specified endpoint product and policy. The earlier claim that downloading or running a local model would itself trigger an immediate alert therefore remains unmeasured.

The Unit 42 and GTIG reports may concern overlapping time periods or actors. Neither report supplies identifiers sufficient to count them as independent confirmation of the *same* incident or to add their campaign totals. Their value here is the documented form of operation and the precise boundaries of the observations.

## New evidence about repeated revision reliability

| Primary source | Result | Boundary for Drexel |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Feng et al., *FeatureLoop*, arXiv 26 October 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35488) | The authors constructed 61 human-reviewed, staged software tasks from 34 repositories, with cumulative executable checks. Across 12 frontier models, the strongest completed 44/61 tasks (72.1%) when both requirement and corrective feedback were available. Across models, 113/733 observed failed stages (15.4%) recovered within two corrective revisions. | A persistent *workspace* with successive instructions and checks, not a self-editing live program. The 15.4% denominator is failed stages, not all tasks, and cannot be used as a malware revision probability. It directly reinforces the need to count attempts, repair failures, and preserved prior behavior across steps. |
| [Huang et al., *Regression Accumulation in Multi-Turn LLM Programming Conversations*, July 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.01855v1) | Six models were tested on 542 short Python tasks, each with an eight-turn requirement sequence. The paper reports that 40%–73% of tasks lost previously correct behavior over the full sequence, depending on model. Its independent prior-test check with rollback and one retry improved final-turn quality in its tested conditions. | This is controlled, function-level conversational code generation. It neither models a remote-access program nor measures continuity of a live process. The percentages should not be multiplied or transferred to the Drexel setting. They show why a successful single edit is weak evidence for repeated retained function. |
| [Ren et al., *Evaluation-Free Recursive Self-Improvement for Coding Agents*, arXiv 16 November 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21056) | The authors report iterative changes to a coding agent's executable implementation and an 85.6% SWE-bench Pro score versus 60.0% for its initial version under their configuration. Candidate selection did not use downstream task evaluations during evolution, but deterministic structural and operational checks were used; the final evaluation used downstream tasks. | Evidence that repeated model-directed software evolution is feasible in a selected benign agent setting. Its descendants and task scores are not one continuously serving implant or access checks after every accepted revision. “Evaluation-free” does not mean unverified runtime survival. |
| [Irregular, *Agentic Self-Modification in Open-Weights Systems*, 16 September 2026](https://irregular.cv/research/agentic-self-modification-in-open-weights-systems) | In a favorable benign maintenance setup, a coding agent fine-tuned and deployed a shared model checkpoint without explicit instruction to modify it; a fresh process using the revised model answered 20/20 held-out fictional-language queries versus 0/20 for the original. The researchers say the setup was designed to demonstrate a mechanism, not estimate its frequency. | This is a **model-weight** change affecting later instances, not payload-code revision in one continuing program. It illustrates that available tools and deployment permissions shape what an agent changes, while making no claim about remote-access continuity. |

The two reliability studies use different populations and outcomes. Their percentages are not directly comparable, and no joint probability of repeated successful Drexel revisions can be calculated from them. The new self-improvement studies establish that agents can alter deployed software or models in controlled settings; they do not remove the need for independent tests of the specific properties that must survive each change.

## Public research lead with insufficient methods

[Black Hat's October 2026 SecTor overview](https://blackhat.com/html/blog/2026-10-05.html) names a briefing, “Large Local Malware: Weaponizing Your Local LLM for Runtime Malware Generation.” The organizer's overview establishes that such a briefing was presented, but provides no original trace, sample denominator, before-and-after payload artifacts, or per-revision access checks. It is a lead for a future primary-material audit, not an observed Drexel result. A conference title alone is especially vulnerable to conflating *fresh runtime code generation* with *repeated revision of a continuing payload*.

## Updated feasibility judgment and paper implications

The technical ingredients are increasingly credible in separate settings: field agents can create and revise scripts quickly; benign agents can repeatedly change executable code; local or hosted inference can support external orchestration; and web shells or other access mechanisms can persist while operators issue new instructions. The crucial combined observation still has not been published in the sources reviewed here. Inference from these ingredients to one self-editing remote-access implant would be a **feasibility hypothesis**, not a demonstrated incident.

For the eventual paper, the strongest defensible contribution is a claim-level evidence map rather than a prevalence or success-rate estimate. It should keep five dimensions separate: (1) where the model runs, (2) whether the model changes commands, scripts, configuration, weights, or resident payload code, (3) who applies the change, (4) whether the same process or merely a service/lineage continues, and (5) whether access is independently checked after each accepted revision. The new field evidence especially argues against treating many unique scripts or rapidly iterated web shells as proof of one self-revising implant. The separate four-gate local-model study remains unfinished and supplies no quality-preservation claim.

### Still needed from public evidence

- One time-ordered incident or controlled trace tying two model outputs to two accepted payload-code revisions in one continuing program.
- Independent confirmation of remote access and task function after each revision, with failed proposals and rejected revisions counted.
- Clear model and controller provenance so operator edits, generated commands, and new deployments are not mislabeled as autonomous self-revision.
- A specified security environment and actual alerts before drawing any detection conclusion.

These are evidence criteria for future source review. No new model test is proposed or reported in this update.
